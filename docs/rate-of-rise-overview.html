Rate of Rise

Warning arrives before the water does.

A solar-powered radar stream gauge on a creek with no official USGS gauge, fused with twelve live data sources into a flood-probability model — a DIY flood early-warning system for a small, flashy basin in northeastern Pennsylvania, built on Home Assistant. It exists to buy back the tens of minutes between rain falling upstream and water reaching the door.

Instrument phase: deployed & operational

The Moteino M0 + RFM69HW creek node went onto the pole on 2026‑09‑19 and has run continuously since, reporting every 60 seconds with stable battery voltage and a sustained −72 dBm RSSI — well inside the −80 dBm link-margin target, with effectively zero packet loss across real rainfall events. Defensive firmware handling (explicit radio reset pulses, re-initialization on wake, flash busy-wait fixes) keeps it running unattended.

24+ hours of field data already show the sensor tracking rainfall-driven water-level changes. The pole was subsequently raised to buy overbank headroom for model training and reduce debris-strike risk; the sensor geometry below reflects the last full survey and is due for a re-measurement at the new height.

Build order: instrument → ingest → collect & correlate → predict → harden. Ingest through predict are code-complete; what's left is real storms, not code.

The gap this fills

Why an off-the-shelf alert does not exist

There is no USGS gauge on this creek. The nearest instruments that publish continuous readings are off-basin or downstream — useful for validating a lag estimate, useless as a stand-in for how high the water is right now.

It is also a flashy basin: roughly 18 mi², and rainfall-to-crest is measured in tens of minutes, not hours. That short fuse is the entire design constraint. A system that reports a flood is a logger. This one is built to say a flood is becoming likely, early enough that the answer is still to move things rather than to bail them out. A prior 100-year storm put roughly 40 inches of water into the basement this system now watches for.

Four layers, one signal path

Sensor to phone, entirely self-hosted

Instrument

A gauge where none existed

An 80 GHz FMCW radar (DFRobot SEN0676) on a creekside pole reads the water surface to ±5 mm over Modbus RTU into a Moteino M0, which transmits on an encrypted 915 MHz point-to-point link to an ESP32 gateway at the house — no WiFi at the creek, no cellular, no subscription. Solar-powered, ~25 mA average. Deployed and reporting continuously since 2026-09-19. Adaptive crest sampling — dropping from 60 s to 5 s on a confirmed rise — is code-complete and waiting on a firmware push to the node.

Ingest

Twelve sources, one feature row +1 source

The rate_of_rise Home Assistant add-on polls twelve sources every fast loop — on-site rain, temperature and soil probes; upstream rain gauges; NWS forecasts and alerts; the National Water Model; the WPC Excessive Rainfall Outlook; NEXRAD storm-cell tracks; SNODAS snowpack; USGS reference gauges; and, newest, Google's Flood Forecasting API — assembling 49 features every cycle, all auto-provisioned as Home Assistant entities over MQTT discovery.

Correlate & predict

Learns this basin specifically

Storm events are logged automatically and the rainfall→response relationship is fit to this catchment rather than borrowed from a regional average. The storm log has already cleared the minimum event count for machine-learning training; the xgboost pipeline is code-complete and unit-tested against synthetic storms, but has not yet trained on a real creek response. It runs on transparent threshold rules until that happens, and says which of the two produced the number on screen.

Alert

Five tiers and an operator console new

A five-level tier ladder drives phone pushes over MQTT, escalating at Watch and above to a critical Android alarm-stream notification that sounds through silent and Do Not Disturb. Any official NWS flood product floors the tier independently of the model. An operator dashboard can trigger inference, retrain, promote or roll back the model, and annotate storms by hand — all sent as MQTT commands the add-on picks up and reports back on.

Operator console

MQTT commands, driven from the Home Assistant dashboard

The modeling service is headless by design — no ports, no ingress — but it isn't hands-off. Five commands, issued as MQTT messages from dashboard buttons, reach the add-on's command queue and publish a result back to a "Last command" sensor:

run_inference

Force an immediate scoring pass instead of waiting for the next fast loop.

retrain

Refit the xgboost candidate against the current dataset on demand.

promote

Activate a candidate model, with a warning if it was never scored.

rollback

Undo a promotion — including the very first one, which never used to be undoable.

annotate

Write a free-text note onto the most recently closed storm event.

Five tiers, and one that wakes you

Escalation ladder — each rung is the lowest condition that reaches it

0
All-clear
No elevated risk. Nothing fires.
1
Advisory
Forecast rain onto already-wet ground, a WPC excessive-rain risk, or an above-normal status on a Google-modeled neighboring gauge.
2
Watch
Rain measured upstream, radar cells inbound, or a severe/extreme Google gauge status or flash-flood polygon over the site. Critical push from here up.
3
Warning
The creek is answering — 24 in stage, or rising 0.05 in/min, confirmed against radio dropouts.
4
Emergency
30 in and climbing — overbank imminent.

At Watch and above the notification routes through Android's alarm stream, so it sounds at alarm volume through silent, vibrate and Do Not Disturb. Any official NWS flood product raises the floor independently — a forecaster knows things two buried probes do not. A dropped radio link to the creek node can no longer manufacture a false Warning: rate of rise is only computed from samples that can be honestly differenced, with the link checked first and the first readings after a reconnect requiring confirmation.

What this does not claim

The honest part

Every threshold in the tier ladder is a placeholder until real storms say otherwise. As of 2026-09-20, most of the project's open engineering questions are closed — the instrument is deployed, the radio link margin is confirmed, solar and charging are settled, the soil probes are field-calibrated, and Google Flood Forecasting is built and verified. What's left is three field-calibration items — the tier thresholds themselves, the antecedent-precipitation recession constant, and the rain-on-snow flag — and no amount of code shortens that wait: they get measured by watching it rain. Every alert this system sends still carries its own disclaimer: thresholds are not yet field-calibrated — verify before acting.

The signal path is complete and tested end to end, and it is now running on real hardware at the creek. This is a personal, best-effort early-warning aid, never a substitute for official NWS/NOAA flood warnings. Do not rely on it as a sole source of flood safety information.
